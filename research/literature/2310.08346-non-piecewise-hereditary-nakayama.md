# Non-piecewise hereditary Nakayama algebras

**arXiv:** [2310.08346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08346)

Asks when `kA_n / J` is piecewise hereditary, and gives criteria showing it often
is not. For us this is the tool that says **a class is not a quipu class**, with
no search.

## Why it matters here

An algebra is *piecewise hereditary* if `D^b(Λ) ≃ D^b(H)` for a hereditary abelian
category `H`. By Happel's classification such an `H` is either the module category
of a hereditary algebra or derived equivalent to a canonical algebra. So:

- not piecewise hereditary ⟹ derived equivalent to **no** hereditary algebra ⟹ in
  no quipu class;
- piecewise hereditary but not of tree type ⟹ should be of **canonical** type, and
  the Coxeter polynomial names the weights.

That is the trichotomy of H-006, and it named every n = 9 class (F-011).

## Results we use

### Counts (from the introduction)

- Every `kA_n / J` with **n ≤ 8** is piecewise hereditary.
- Among the 1430 quotients of `kA_9` there is **precisely one** that is not,
  namely `(**)`: relations 1→4, 3→6, 4→7, 6→9. In our notation `3033030`.

Both are used as tests: our criteria must certify nothing below length 9 and
exactly that one algebra at length 9. This is the strongest check we have on the
implementation.

### Proposition A9 (`proposition:A9extended`)

At least 9 vertices, and relations `α` (leftmost) and `β` overlapping by **≥ 2
arrows**, with:

1. no relation starting at the vertex directly before `α`'s start and ending at or
   before the vertex directly after `β`'s start;
2. no relation ending at the vertex directly after `β`'s end and starting at or
   after the vertex directly before `α`'s end;
3. some relation of length **≥ 3** lying entirely before `β` (may share a vertex);
4. some relation of length ≥ 3 lying entirely after `α` (may share a vertex).

Then not piecewise hereditary. Loosely: *an overlapping pair flanked by a long
relation on each side.* Implemented as `failsFlankedPairCriterion`.

### Proposition A13 (`proposition:A13tworelations`)

Relations `α` (leftmost) and `β` overlapping by **≥ 6 arrows**, with at least two
vertices between the two starts and at least two between the two ends, and no
relation from the first-or-second vertex before `β`'s start to the first-or-second
vertex after `α`'s end. Then not piecewise hereditary.

Smallest instance: `A_13` with 1→10 and 4→13. Implemented as
`failsBigOverlapCriterion`.

### Corollary `removevertex`

If `Λ` is piecewise hereditary, so is `Γ` obtained by deleting a vertex `i` —
adding a composite arrow if `i` has both an arrow in and out, and **extending** the
relations that start or end at `i`.

Used contrapositively, and downward: if some one-vertex deletion of `Λ` is
certified, so is `Λ` (F-012, `notPiecewiseHereditaryByDeletion`).

### Corollary `introducevertex` — and why we do not use it directly

The same result read forwards: add a vertex, with constrained choices of relations,
and non-piecewise-heredity is preserved. **Extensions are not unique** — many
algebras of length `n+1` restrict to a given one of length `n` — so going up means
enumerating a branching set of possibilities, while going down is one deterministic
algebra per vertex. Hence the downward recursion.

### Proposition `doubleMutation` — the double tilting mutation
*read 2026-09-17* · **implemented as `quivermutation/doubleMutation.py`, F-032**

The paper's framing, worth keeping: a *single* tilting mutation seldom gives a
derived equivalence between Nakayama algebras, and the ones that do are already
covered by `corollary:lengthtworelations`; mutating **twice** gives interesting
ones. Stated verbatim in substance:

> Let `r` be a relation from vertex `s` to `t`, such that there is another
> relation starting in `s - 1`, but no relation starting in `t - 1`. Then `Λ` is
> derived equivalent to the Nakayama algebra obtained from it by
>
> - if `t` is not the final vertex, **adding** a new relation, of the same length
>   as `r`, from `s + 1` to `t + 1`;
> - **shortening at the start** every relation starting properly within `r`
>   (in a vertex `r` passes through, not `s` or `t`);
> - **lengthening toward its end** any relation ending properly within `r` —
>   "this will generally include the second relation from the assumption".

The result is called `L_t(Λ)`, "since it is the result of left mutation"; the
dual `R_s(Λ)` is named and not stated. "Left mutation tends to move relations to
the right, and vice versa." The paper's remark: *found through computer
experiments using [Fosse2021], after which a direct proof was easy.*

**The proof is two left mutations at `t`**, so in the repo's convention the
sequence is `[-t, -t]` and this is a *mutation* equivalence, usable wherever a
rule is. First step: no relation starts at `t - 1`, so `P_t → P_{t-1}` is mono
with cokernel `S_{t-1}`. Second: by `r`, `S_{t-1}` is the socle of `P_s` and of
no later projective, giving the approximation `S_{t-1} → P_s` with cokernel
`P_s / S_{t-1}`. The relation at `s - 1` is used for exactly one thing: to make
`P_s / S_{t-1}` map to `P_{s-1}`, so it slots in between `P_s` and `P_{s-1}` and
the quiver is a line again.

**In the repo's terms.** It is *not* a `(width, before, after, sequence)` rule
and cannot be one. By minimality no relation lies inside another, so every
relation the proposition changes starts or ends inside `r` and runs out past its
far end — it straddles any window around `r`, which `lnaMoves.matchesAt` refuses.
It is a family over **every configuration of relations crossing `r`**, with one
fixed sequence. The table's rules are its instances with nothing crossing:

| instance | what it is in the repo |
|---|---|
| companion `s-1 → t-1`, nothing else, `t < n` | the pair slide, `pairSlideRules` |
| companion `s-1 → t-1`, `t = n` | the sink half of `endPairCollapseRules` (F-022) |
| `s = 1`, no companion, nothing starting in `r` | `edgeMoves.sourceDoubling` (F-029) |

So F-022's end collapse and F-029's doubling were in the literature all along —
the second only by reading the proof rather than the statement, see below.

**It is not interior in the sense that would contradict H-010.** On an isolated
pair it slides the pair and nothing else; the overlap is conserved. What it adds
is that the slide carries any number of *bystanders* with it, lengthening and
shortening them, and that is what F-023 found blocking the rules.

**Two readings of our own, both checked against the engine, neither in the
paper.**

1. *The `s = 1` extension.* At the source there is no `P_0` for the new vertex to
   slot in front of, and the quiver is a line regardless, so the hypothesis "a
   relation starts at `s - 1`" is not needed there. 428 cases at `n = 9` and 1429
   at `n = 10` in each direction, no failures. It turns out to add no coverage
   beyond the dual of the paper's `t = n` case (F-032).
2. *Minimality.* Lengthening can make a relation contain another — the
   companion ending at `t - 1` lengthens onto `r` — and it is then dropped.

## Other results in the paper not yet used

- **`lemma:taupathimpliesnotpwh`** — a path `X[i] ⇝ X` built from
  Auslander–Reiten translates certifies non-piecewise-heredity, and the examples
  apply it as `τ^k(P_i) = P_i[j]`. It is **stronger than A9 and A13** for us:
  `example:A10double` uses it to show `34504030` and `50505000` are not piecewise
  hereditary, and neither of our implemented criteria certifies either (E-029).
  That pair is the one polynomial group at `n = 10` shared with a quipu, so this
  lemma is what settles it. Implementing `τ` on `D^b` of an LNA is the natural
  next certificate.
- **`remark:Coxeter`** — those two share `T^10 + T^9 + T + 1` with the piecewise
  hereditary `kA_10 / rad^9`.
- **`liste:baseforHS`** and `proposition:HS` — `kA_11/rad^4`, `kA_11/rad^5`,
  `kA_12/rad^3`, `kA_12/rad^6`, `kA_12/rad^7` are not piecewise hereditary;
  Happel–Seidel's Table 1 recovered without algebraic closedness.
- **`corollary:introducevertexatend`** and `corollary:NonOverlappingRelSets` —
  not yet read closely.

## Caveats and limitations

- The criteria are **sufficient, not a characterisation**. A negative result means
  only that these criteria say nothing. All our counts of non-piecewise-hereditary
  algebras are therefore lower bounds (H-004).
- Deleting a vertex preserves piecewise heredity **only** — not the derived
  equivalence class, the Coxeter polynomial, or the quipu. The certificate is
  purely negative and must never be used to merge two classes.
- The paper notes that two LNAs can share a Coxeter polynomial with one piecewise
  hereditary and the other not (its `remark:Coxeter`), so the Happel–Seidel style
  argument via a *list* of Coxeter polynomials cannot be relied on in general.
- The introduction's framing is worth keeping in mind: the scarcity of
  non-piecewise-hereditary examples at small `n` is an **artefact of the sizes
  computer search can reach**, not a fact about Nakayama algebras.
