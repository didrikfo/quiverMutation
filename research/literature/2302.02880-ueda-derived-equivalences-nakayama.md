# On derived equivalences of Nakayama algebras

**arXiv:** [2302.02880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02880) · T. Ueda · 6 Feb 2023
· *read 2026-09-18 from the arXiv PDF*

Produces derived equivalences between **radical-power LNAs of the same `n` and
adjacent `ℓ`** — `N(n, ℓ+1) ≃ N(n, ℓ)` — which is a merge no other source gives in
that generality, plus an explicit derived equivalence from every `N(n,ℓ)` to an
algebra of global dimension ≤ 2. Read it for the two corollaries; the machinery
(`S`-families, mutations of exceptional sequences) is a technique for producing
tilting objects and we do not need it to *use* the results.

`N(n, ℓ) = KA_n / (rad KA_n)^ℓ` with `A_n` linearly oriented `1 → 2 → … → n`, i.e.
exactly our `"ℓ" * (n − ℓ) + "0" * (ℓ − 2)`. Field `K` arbitrary — **no algebraic
closedness assumed anywhere**, which is unusual in this literature and matters,
since arXiv:2310.08346 makes a point of it.

## Results we use

### Corollary 1.3 = Corollary 5.15 — the adjacent-radical-power merge

> Let `p, q` be integers with `p ≥ 2`, `q ≥ 1`, and suppose one of
> - (a) `r ∈ Z_{≥0}`, or
> - (b) `p = 2` and `r ∈ ½·Z_{≥0}` (so `r` may be a half-integer),
>
> holds. Then there is a triangle equivalence
> `per N(n, ℓ+1) → per N(n, ℓ)` where
> `n = p(p+1)q + p(p−1)r` and `ℓ = (p+1)q + pr`.

Concretely: enumerate `(p,q,r)` in the allowed ranges, compute `(n, ℓ)`, and
**`"ℓ+1"*(n−ℓ−1) + "0"*(ℓ−1)` is derived equivalent to `"ℓ"*(n−ℓ) + "0"*(ℓ−2)`**.
Smallest instances:

| `p,q,r` | `n` | `ℓ` | merge |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2,1,0 | 6 | 3 | `N_6(4) ≃ N_6(3)`, i.e. `4400 ≃ 3330` |
| 2,1,½ | 7 | 4 | `N_7(5) ≃ N_7(4)`, i.e. `55000 ≃ 44400` |
| 2,2,0 | 12 | 6 | `N_12(7) ≃ N_12(6)` |
| 3,1,0 | 12 | 4 | `N_12(5) ≃ N_12(4)` |
| 2,1,1 | 8 | 5 | `N_8(6) ≃ N_8(5)` |

Note (b) is the `p = 2` special case that yields the whole family
`per N(s + 6q, s + 3q + 1) → per N(s + 6q, s + 3q)` for `q ≥ 1`, `s ≥ 0`. For
`q = 1` this is Happel–Seidel [9, Prop. 2.3]; for `q = 2` it is
Lenzing–Meltzer–Ruan [15, Thm. 6.3]; for `q ≥ 3` it appears to be new with this
paper.

Direction of the arrow is immaterial — `per` equivalences are invertible.

### Corollary 1.6 = Corollary 5.17 — the transpose merge

> `per N(pq + 1, q + 1) ≃ per N(pq + 1, p + 1)` for all integers `p, q ≥ 2`.

This is exactly part (3) of Lenzing–Meltzer–Ruan's extended Happel–Seidel symmetry
(`a = q+1`, `b = p+1`, `n = (a−1)(b−1) + 1`), reproved without weighted projective
lines and **without algebraic closedness**. Ueda attributes the statement to
[15, Prop. 4.1]. Use whichever proof you prefer; the statement is the same.

### Theorem 1.1 = Proposition 4.2 + Theorem 4.7 — the gl.dim ≤ 2 model

> For all `s, t ≥ 1` and `0 ≤ u ≤ t`, there are triangle equivalences
> `per N(st − u, t + 1) → per L(s,t,u) → per L!(s,t,u)`.
> In particular `N(st − u, t + 1)` is derived equivalent to a `K`-algebra of
> global dimension ≤ 2.

Here, with `e_i ⊗ e_j` the idempotent at vertex `(i,j)`,

    L(s,t,u)  = (N(s,2)^op ⊗ N(t,2)^op) / ⟨ Σ_{i=0}^{u−1} e_s ⊗ e_{t−i} ⟩
    L!(s,t,u) = (K A_s ⊗ K A_t)        / ⟨ Σ_{i=0}^{u−1} e_s^! ⊗ e_{t−i}^! ⟩

and `L(s,t,t) = L(s−1,t,0)`. For `u = 0` this recovers Ladkani's
`per N(st, t+1) ≃ per(KA_s ⊗ KA_t)` (Ladkani, *On derived equivalences of lines,
rectangles and triangles*, J. LMS 87 (2013), Cor. 1.2) — the line-to-rectangle
equivalence already recreated in arXiv:2112.08129.

Restated as a recipe: **every `N(n, ℓ)` with `n ≥ 3`, `ℓ ≥ 2` is derived equivalent
to `L(⌈n/(ℓ−1)⌉, ℓ−1, r)` with `r = (ℓ−1)⌈n/(ℓ−1)⌉ − n`.** So the derived class of
a radical-power LNA has a canonical representative on a **two-dimensional grid
quiver with commutativity relations**, not a line. If we ever want to compare
radical-power classes to each other by something other than the Coxeter
polynomial, these grid algebras are a uniform model to compare.

### Theorem 1.2 = Theorem 5.14 — the underlying grid statement

> Let `s, t, u` be positive integers with `1 ≤ u ≤ t`. Suppose
> - (a) `u ∈ Zs` and `t − u ∈ Z(s+1)`, or
> - (b) `s = 2` and `t − u ∈ 3Z`.
>
> Then `per L(s,t,u) ≃ per L(s, t−1, u−s)`.

Corollary 1.3 is this plus Theorem 1.1, with `(s,t,u) = (p, (p+1)q + pr, pr)`.
Worth recording separately because the grid statement has a *wider* hypothesis set
than the Nakayama corollary reads off it: condition (a) is a pair of divisibility
conditions on `(s,t,u)`, and only the particular parametrisation
`(p, (p+1)q + pr, pr)` is turned into Nakayama language. **If you want more merges,
enumerate `(s,t,u)` satisfying (a) or (b) directly and push both sides back through
Theorem 1.1** — each side is a Nakayama algebra only when `u` is such that
`st − u` and `s(t−1) − (u−s) = st − u` agree, which they do, so both sides are
`N(st − u, ·)` with `t+1` and `t` respectively. That is the same family; the extra
generality is in `(s,t,u)` not being of the form `(p,(p+1)q+pr,pr)` — check
whether condition (a) has solutions outside that parametrisation before assuming
it does. (The paper asserts that the integers satisfying (a) *can be written* as
`(p, (p+1)q + pr, pr)`, so probably not; (b) is the genuinely extra case.)

### Theorem 1.5 = Theorem 5.16

> `per L(p+1, q, q−1) ≃ per L(q+1, p, p−1)` for `p, q ≥ 2`.

The grid statement behind Corollary 1.6; the symmetry is visibly the transpose of
the Young diagram.

## Lemmas worth knowing

- **Theorem 1.4 = Theorem 3.11.** If `D` is algebraic, idempotent-complete,
  Ext-finite with a Serre functor and contains a *full `S`-family* `(X_{i,j})`,
  then `D ≃ per L(S)` with `X_{i,j} ↦ P(i,j)`. This is the engine: to prove two
  things derived equivalent, exhibit an `S`-family in one of them. If we ever
  want to prove a new merge ourselves rather than quote one, this is a usable
  general tool and it is elementary (conditions (S1)–(S3), Definition 3.8).
- The convention note in §1 matters when checking anything by hand: modules are
  **right** modules, and for a source `i` in `Q`, `S(i) ≃ P(i)`. Our `A_n` is
  `1 → 2 → … → n`, so vertex 1 is the source.

## Caveats and limitations

- **Radical powers only.** Every statement is about `KA_n / rad^ℓ`. Nothing here
  touches an LNA with two different relation lengths, which is the case we care
  about.
- **Corollary 1.3's parameters are not a complete answer.** It produces *some*
  pairs `(n, ℓ+1), (n, ℓ)`; it says nothing about the pairs it misses, and in
  particular gives no non-equivalence. Figure 1 of the paper (an `(n, ℓ)` scatter
  with arrows) makes the sparsity visible.
- The paper works in `per`, the perfect derived category, not `D^b(mod)`. For an
  LNA these agree — a linear quiver is directed so `gl.dim < ∞` and every bounded
  complex is perfect — but do not carry the statements over to the *cyclic*
  Nakayama algebras, where they do not.
- **`L(s,t,u)` is not a Nakayama algebra** and not an LNA. Theorem 1.1 moves the
  problem out of our world; it is useful as a normal form, not as a merge between
  two things we enumerate.
- Ladkani's Cor. 1.2 as quoted requires `st > t + 1`; Ueda's generalisation drops
  that, but the `u = 0` specialisation only recovers Ladkani in that range.

## What it does not give us

- No invariant at all — the paper never separates two algebras, only merges.
- No statement about piecewise heredity, and no contact with quipus.
- No local move on the digit string: like the Happel–Seidel symmetry, the merges
  here change `ℓ` by one and therefore change every digit.

## How it lands on our problem

Corollary 1.3 and Corollary 1.6 are two families of merge rules on digit strings
of the shape `d^k 0^m`, checkable immediately against a finished classification at
`n ≤ 10` and usable as seeds above it. Corollary 1.3 is the one to implement: it is
the only source we have for `N_n(ℓ+1) ≃ N_n(ℓ)` beyond the small sporadic cases in
Lenzing–Meltzer–Ruan's Figure 1, and it extends to arbitrarily large `n` in closed
form.
